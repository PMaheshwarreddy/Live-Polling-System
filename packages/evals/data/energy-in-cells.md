# Energy in Cells: Photosynthesis and Respiration

Living things need a steady supply of energy to grow, move and repair themselves. Plants, algae and some bacteria capture energy from sunlight through photosynthesis. Almost every organism, including plants, releases the energy stored in food through cellular respiration. The two processes are linked: the products of one are the raw materials of the other.

## Photosynthesis overview

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. The overall equation is: carbon dioxide plus water, using light energy, produces glucose and oxygen. In symbols this is 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2. Carbon dioxide enters the leaf through tiny pores called stomata, and water travels up from the roots through the xylem.

Photosynthesis takes place inside chloroplasts. A chloroplast is surrounded by a double membrane. Inside are stacks of flattened discs called thylakoids, and the fluid around them is called the stroma. The green pigment chlorophyll sits in the thylakoid membranes. Chlorophyll absorbs mostly red and blue light and reflects green light, which is why leaves look green.

## The light-dependent reactions

The light-dependent reactions happen in the thylakoid membranes. When chlorophyll absorbs light, its electrons gain energy and are passed along an electron transport chain. To replace those electrons, water molecules are split in a process called photolysis. Splitting water releases oxygen gas as a by-product, so the oxygen we breathe comes from water, not from carbon dioxide.

The energy from the electrons is used to make two energy carriers: ATP and NADPH. These molecules do not stay in the thylakoids. They move into the stroma, where they power the second stage of photosynthesis.

## The Calvin cycle

The Calvin cycle, also called the light-independent reactions, takes place in the stroma. It does not need light directly, but it depends on the ATP and NADPH made in the light-dependent reactions. In the first step, called carbon fixation, the enzyme RuBisCO attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon sugar called RuBP. RuBisCO is thought to be the most abundant protein on Earth.

The products are then reduced using energy from ATP and electrons from NADPH, forming a three-carbon sugar called G3P. Some G3P leaves the cycle and is used to build glucose, sucrose and starch. The rest is recycled to regenerate RuBP so the cycle can continue. It takes six turns of the Calvin cycle to make one molecule of glucose.

## Factors that limit photosynthesis

The rate of photosynthesis depends on light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration and temperature. A limiting factor is whichever of these is in shortest supply; increasing it will speed up photosynthesis until another factor becomes limiting. On a cold, bright morning, temperature is often the limiting factor. Because photosynthesis is controlled by enzymes, very high temperatures denature those enzymes and the rate falls sharply. Greenhouse farmers raise carbon dioxide levels and use artificial lighting to increase crop yields.

## Cellular respiration overview

Cellular respiration releases the energy stored in glucose and stores it in ATP, the energy currency of the cell. The overall equation for aerobic respiration is the reverse of photosynthesis: glucose plus oxygen produces carbon dioxide, water and energy. Respiration happens all the time in every living cell, day and night, including in plant cells.

## Glycolysis

The first stage of respiration is glycolysis, which takes place in the cytoplasm and does not need oxygen. Glycolysis splits one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate. This stage produces a net gain of only two ATP molecules and two NADH molecules.

## The Krebs cycle and the electron transport chain

When oxygen is available, pyruvate enters the mitochondria. In the matrix of the mitochondrion, the Krebs cycle (also called the citric acid cycle) breaks pyruvate down completely, releasing carbon dioxide as a waste product. The Krebs cycle makes a small amount of ATP, but its main job is to load electron carriers such as NADH and FADH2.

These carriers deliver electrons to the electron transport chain on the inner mitochondrial membrane. As electrons pass along the chain, protons are pumped across the membrane, and the protons flow back through an enzyme called ATP synthase, which makes ATP. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor: it combines with electrons and protons to form water. Without oxygen the chain stops. Aerobic respiration yields about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose molecule, most of it from the electron transport chain. The folded inner membrane, called the cristae, gives a large surface area for this process.

## Anaerobic respiration and fermentation

When oxygen is scarce, cells can still make a little ATP through glycolysis, but they must recycle NADH to keep glycolysis running. In human muscle cells during hard exercise, pyruvate is converted into lactic acid. This lactic acid fermentation causes the burning feeling in tired muscles, and the extra oxygen needed afterwards to break the lactic acid down is called the oxygen debt.

Yeast and plant cells carry out alcoholic fermentation instead, turning pyruvate into ethanol and carbon dioxide. Bakers rely on the carbon dioxide to make bread rise, and brewers rely on the ethanol. Anaerobic respiration produces only 2 ATP per glucose, far less than aerobic respiration.

## Comparing the two processes

Photosynthesis stores energy and respiration releases it. Photosynthesis happens in chloroplasts and only in the light; respiration happens in the cytoplasm and mitochondria at all times. Photosynthesis uses carbon dioxide and water and releases oxygen; aerobic respiration uses oxygen and releases carbon dioxide and water. A common misconception is that plants photosynthesise instead of respiring. In fact plants do both, and at night, when there is no light, plants only respire, so they release carbon dioxide.

The light compensation point is the light intensity at which the rate of photosynthesis exactly equals the rate of respiration, so there is no net exchange of gases.
